Think Before You Share: Critical Thinking and Media Literacy

Every day we receive a lot of information on our phones. Many of us have seen a message in a family group chat that says, “Share this before they delete it!” Sometimes the message is true, but very often it is not. That is why critical thinking and media literacy are so important today.

What do these words mean?

Critical thinking means asking questions and judging evidence before we accept an idea. Media literacy is the ability to find, understand and evaluate the information that comes from newspapers, television, websites and social networks.

Five questions I ask before I believe or share something

  1. Who wrote it? Is the author a real person or an organization with a good reputation?
  2. What is the source? Does the text mention official data, documents or experts?
  3. When was it published? An old story can look like new news.
  4. Do other reliable media say the same? I always try to compare at least two sources.
  5. How does it make me feel? Fake news often uses anger or fear to make us react quickly.

A lesson from my job

In accounting, I never accept a number without a supporting document. If an invoice does not have the correct information, I cannot record it. I think we should use the same rule with the news: no evidence, no trust. If we learn to check information, we will protect ourselves and the people around us. If we do not, we may help fake news to spread.

For example, a message may say that a bank will close all accounts tomorrow unless you forward it to ten contacts. A quick look at the bank’s official website usually shows that the story is false.

What do you do to check the news? Tell me in the comments!

Crime and Punishment: Is Prison the Only Answer?

When we hear about a crime on the news, many people ask for a harder punishment immediately. I understand this reaction, but I believe the topic is more complex. In this post I want to share my opinion about crime and punishment.

Why does crime happen?

There is no single cause. Poverty, lack of education, inequality and corruption can all push people toward crime. Of course, none of these reasons is an excuse, but they help us understand the problem.

What is punishment for?

Societies punish crime for different reasons:

  • to protect people from dangerous criminals,
  • to discourage others from committing the same crime,
  • to give justice to the victims,
  • and to help offenders change their lives.

My opinion

I think a punishment should be fair and proportional to the crime. It should also give the person a chance to improve. If prisoners could study and learn a job, they would be more likely to build a new life when they leave. On the other hand, if a prison only keeps people locked up, the problem may continue.

Crimes with numbers

Because I work in accounting, I also think about financial crimes such as fraud, tax evasion and embezzlement. These crimes do not use violence, but they hurt many people because they steal money from companies, workers and public services. This is why honesty and ethics are essential for every accountant.

In my opinion, the best solution combines three things: prevention, fair punishment and rehabilitation. What do you think? Should societies focus more on punishment or on rehabilitation?

My Training Program at SENA

SENA (Servicio Nacional de Aprendizaje) is a Colombian public institution that offers technical and technological training. Thousands of people in my country have improved their careers thanks to it, and I am one of them.

What am I studying?

I am a student in the Tecnólogo en Gestión Contable y de Información Financiera program (group number 3335860), which belongs to the Centro de Biotecnología Agropecuaria in the Girardot regional office. The program teaches us how to:

  • record and classify accounting transactions,
  • prepare invoices and billing documents,
  • understand taxes and legal obligations,
  • build and read financial statements,
  • and use accounting software.

Why is English part of it?

Accounting is an international language. Companies work with foreign clients, suppliers and rules, so English is a very useful tool. During this course I have practiced reading, writing and speaking in English, and this blog is part of that process.

Studying while working

It is not easy to work and study at the same time, but it has an advantage: I can use what I learn in class at my job the next day. Planning my time and being disciplined has been the key. My favorite part of the program is working with real accounting software, because I can practice what I will do in an office. Recently, I have learned more about how to check bank statements and match them with the company’s records.

In the future, I would like to continue growing in the financial area and keep improving my English.

A Day at Monserrate, Bogotá

If you visit Bogotá, there is a place you cannot miss: Monserrate. It is a mountain that rises above the city, about 3,152 meters above sea level. At the top there is a church dedicated to the Fallen Lord, and it has been an important place for pilgrims and visitors for a long time.

How to get there

There are three ways to go up: by cable car, by funicular railway or on foot. Walking is for people who are in good physical condition, because the path is steep and the air is thinner at this altitude.

What can you do there?

  • Enjoy a wide view of Bogotá and the mountains around it.
  • Visit the sanctuary and the gardens.
  • Try traditional Colombian food at the restaurants and market stalls.
  • Take lots of photos!

My tips

  • Go in the morning, when the sky is usually clearer.
  • Wear comfortable shoes and bring a jacket, because it can be cold and windy.
  • Drink water and walk slowly if you are not used to the altitude.

What I like most about Monserrate is the view. From the top you can see how big Bogotá really is, and for a few minutes the noise of the city seems very far away. Bogotá is a big, busy city, but Monserrate shows us a calm and beautiful side of it. If you have the chance, I recommend that you go.
